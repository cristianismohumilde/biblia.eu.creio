Biblical Studies

Why these languages matter

A practical overview for textual criticism, exegesis, and transmission history.

Hebrew

Hebrew is the base language of most of the Old Testament. It is essential for preserving semantic nuance, poetic structure, and theological vocabulary at the source level.

Aramaic

Aramaic appears in parts of the Old Testament and in early Jewish traditions. It helps trace linguistic transitions and interpretive bridges within the Semitic world.

Greek

Greek is the language of the New Testament and of the Septuagint. It is key for early interpretive history and for tracking major theological terminology.

Latin

The Vulgate shaped Western Christian theology, liturgy, and scholarship for centuries. Latin remains crucial for historical theology and textual reception.

Ge'ez

The Ethiopic tradition preserves valuable textual witnesses outside the Greco-Latin stream, offering broader comparative depth for biblical transmission.

Syriac

Syriac traditions, especially the Peshitta, are major witnesses of Eastern Christianity and provide strong comparative value with Hebrew and Greek sources.

Coptic

Coptic witnesses are important for reconstructing the Egyptian reception of Scripture and for evaluating textual variants, especially in the New Testament.

Armenian

Ancient Armenian biblical tradition often preserves meaningful readings for variant analysis and historical transmission mapping.

Methodological Note

Using multiple languages is not only about translation diversity. It allows family-level comparison, variant detection, and historically grounded interpretation.
